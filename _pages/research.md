At a Glance: Worlds Around Other Stars
--------------------------------------

{% include toc %}

As the remnants of star formation, planets help tell the story of how solar 
systems like ours form and evolve over their lifetimes. Observations of planets 
both in our solar system and beyond demonstrate an incredible diversity of 
possible planet formation outcomes in terms of mass, radius, temperature, age, 
orbital characteristics, atmospheric and interior compositions and structures, 
host star properties, system architectures, and cosmic birth environments. And 
of course, planets are the only objects known so far to be capable of supporting 
life. However, the census of exoplanets is still largely incomplete, and 
countless questions about our cosmic origins remain unanswered. Just how 
special is our solar system? How did our planet come to be? 
Are we alone?

Astrophysical Habitability
--------------------------

Despite thousands of exoplanet discoveries, no unambiguously habitable exoplanets have been
detected and their formation processes are not fully understood. Current assumptions regarding
the habitability of exoplanets are largely based on our understanding of life on Earth. Namely,
the consensus that carbon-based life on Earth requires water has motivated searches for habitable
planets to focus on identifying environments that can sustain stable liquid water over long (∼Gyr)
timescales. The presence of liquid water would also imply atmospheric pressures and temperatures
that are suitable for biological reactions to occur. As a result, the circumstellar habitable zone (HZ)
has long been defined as the range of star&ndash;planet distances where conditions are neither too hot nor
too cold to allow stable liquid water to exist on a rocky planet's surface. 
The boundaries of the HZ as (defined by Kopparapu et al. 2014) are shown in Fig. 1, along with known 
confirmed exoplanets whose orbits intersect with the optimistic HZ.

However, in reality the boundaries of the HZ are not well-defined. Dozens (likely more) of higher-order
astrophysical effects determine whether any individual planet could be habitable. In no particular 
order and not an exhaustive list, these include planetary atmospheric composition and circulation, 
cloud cover, climate feedback (e.g., the carbonate&ndash;silicate cycle), volcanic outgassing, 
tidal or radiogenic heating, orbital eccentricity and obliquity, and land/ocean fraction. 
<a href="https://scixplorer.org/abs/2018haex.bookE..57M/abstract" target="_blank">Meadows & Barnes (2018)</a> 
provide a nice review of possible factors affecting habitability ranging from stellar effects
(e.g., spectral energy distribution, composition and structure, galactic location) to system-wide
effects (e.g., sibling planets, orbital evolution) to planetary effects (e.g., interior, 
atmosphere, orbital dynamics, surface conditions). 

Much of my Ph.D. dissertation work focused on the former two categories: stellar effects and 
the influence of other planets within the system&mdash;particularly in the context
of preparing for future space-based direct imaging missions that will search for potentially 
habitable Earth-size planets in nearby HZs. One such mission is of course the 
<a href="https://science.nasa.gov/astrophysics/programs/habitable-worlds-observatory/" target="_blank">Habitable Worlds Observatory</a> 
(HWO), which is a NASA mission concept with a target launch date in the 2040s. Potential target stars for HWO's
exoEarth survey are shown in Fig. 2. My research has investigated the stellar properties and known planetary systems 
around many of the Tier 1 targets, which are necessary to inform HWO's overall architecture and eventually 
provide context for HWO's measurements of potential biosignatures on Earth-size HZ planets.

project website**, or check out some of the papers listed below.*

Exomoons
---------

Exomoons (extrasolar moons) are the natural satellites of exoplanets. Similar to
how their Solar System cousins provide clues about the evolutionary 
past of our neighboring planets, exomoons will play an exciting role in 
completing our understanding of exoplanetary systems. As primordial 
remnants of planet formation, byproducts of planetesimal collisions, or 
gravitationally captured interlopers, exomoons must contain important clues 
about how planets form and evolve. Tidal interactions between exomoons and rocky
host planets may also be important for long-term climate stability, therefore 
playing a vital role in habitability. Exomoons themselves could also become
habitable under an appropriate set of conditions.

Despite their scientific promise, exomoons remain elusive to observational 
searches due to their small masses and radii compared to their planetary hosts. 
In most cases, astrophysical false positives and instrumental systematics 
dominate the tiny signals from exomoons in transit and RV observations of 
exoplanets. Two possible exceptions are the exomoon candidates Kepler-1625 b-i
(<a href="https://scixplorer.org/abs/2018SciA....4.1784T/abstract" target="_blank">Teachey & Kipping 2018</a>) 
and Kepler-1708 b-i (<a href="https://scixplorer.org/abs/2022NatAs...6..367K/abstract" target="_blank">Kipping et al. 2022</a>), 
though additional follow-up is necessary to confirm the nature of those signals. 
The problem of detecting exomoons via the transit method is demonstrated by the 
animation in Fig. 3. For this idealized (noiseless) example, the time evolution 
of the transit light curve morphology over 3 epochs is shown in Fig. 4.

Although exomoons cannot yet be robustly detected, we can constrain the 
parameter space of plausible moon scenarios through analytical arguments and 
numerical simulations. HIP 41378 is one interesting multiplanet system for
potential exomoon characterization. In particular, HIP 41378 f is a temperate 
Jovian-size planet (in the optimistic HZ) with an anomalously low mass, giving 
it an extremely low density that challenges conventional planet formation 
theories. One possible explanation is the presence of a circumplanetary ring 
system, which would increases the transit depth we see in the photometric light 
curves observed by K2 and Hubble. Because rings are commonly accompanied by 
moons in the Solar System, HIP 41378 f provides a potential testbed for 
detecting exomoons.

I previously led a paper investigating a range of plausible exomoon scenarios 
that would be consistent with photodynamical transit constraints and a suite
of N-body and tidal evolution simulations. One example of an N-body setup for
this system is shown by the animation in Fig. 5. An example orbital stability 
map resulting from hundreds of simulations is shown in Fig. 6. The parameter
space was explored efficiently using a quadtree data structure, which I 
implemented in the Python package <a href="https://astroqtpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/" target="_blank">astroQTpy</a>. 
The results from these simulations suggest that long-term stable, Earth-sized 
moons around HIP 41378 f would be dynamically viable and consistent with the K2 
and HST data, thus demonstrating the promise of HIP 41378 f as a future 
laboratory for testing theories of exomoon formation and habitability. See the 
publication highlights below to learn more about exomoons and the 
HIP 41378 system.
